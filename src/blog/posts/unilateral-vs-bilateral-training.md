A back squat uses both legs. A split squat uses one leg for most of the work. A barbell bench press uses both arms. A single-arm dumbbell press uses one.

Lifters argue about which style is better. Some say single-limb work fixes imbalances and protects your back. Others say two-limb lifts build more strength and nothing else comes close.

The research gives a clear answer on muscle size and a more detailed answer on strength. This post covers both, then shows how to pick the right style for your goal and your joints. It builds on [The Whole Body Strength Matrix](/blog/whole-body-strength-matrix/), where every session trains four movement patterns: squat, hinge, push, and pull.

## Quick definitions

A **bilateral** exercise uses both limbs at the same time. The back squat, deadlift, and barbell bench press are bilateral.

A **unilateral** exercise works one limb at a time. The split squat, single-leg Romanian deadlift, and single-arm row are unilateral.

The **bilateral deficit** is a drop in force. When you push with both legs at once, each leg often produces a little less force than it can produce alone. Researchers still debate why it happens. One main explanation is that the nervous system sends a weaker signal to each limb when both work together [5]. The effect also varies a lot between people, tasks, and training history [6].

**Specificity** means you get better at what you practice. Train a lift often and you improve at that lift.

**Asymmetry** means one side of your body is stronger or more powerful than the other.

## What the research says

### Muscle size: no clear winner

A 2025 meta-analysis pulled together nine studies that compared unilateral and bilateral training. It found no significant difference in muscle growth between the two [1]. A **meta-analysis** combines the results of many studies into one bigger answer. A larger 2026 analysis covered 24 randomized trials. It also found no significant difference in muscle growth, and none in sprint speed or change of direction [2].

A 12-week trial in 43 young women compared single-leg and two-leg knee extension training. Muscle thickness rose by similar amounts in both groups [3].

If your goal is muscle size, pick the version that lets you train hard and safely. Total weekly work matters more than whether you use one limb or two.

### Strength: you get what you train

Both meta-analyses found the same pattern. Bilateral training produced more gains in bilateral strength. Unilateral training produced more gains in unilateral strength [1][2]. The effect sizes were small to moderate, and the studies carried a moderate risk of bias [1].

An 8-week leg press trial in 28 untrained young men showed the same thing. Both groups improved two-leg strength by a similar amount. The single-leg group improved one-leg strength more [4].

A rugby study with 33 players compared back squats with step-ups. Both groups gained strength in the exercise they trained, and both also improved in the exercise they did not train. Squat strength gains were similar between groups [7]. That result suggests strength carries over between related exercises, even though it is largest in the exact lift you practice.

The same research group looked at sprinting and change of direction. Both styles improved sprint acceleration, and the bilateral group transferred more to change-of-direction ability [8]. One trial cannot settle that question, so treat it as a lead.

### Balance and asymmetry: mixed, and mostly in athletes

Single-limb training has a good case for reducing differences between sides. In a six-week trial of 22 young basketball players, a unilateral group reduced between-limb imbalance more than a bilateral group [9]. In a trial of 66 youth basketball players, single-leg jump training reduced asymmetry, while two-leg jump training increased it [10].

Bilateral training can also help. In a 12-week study of 111 women aged 60 and older, a standard bilateral program reduced leg asymmetry. The weaker leg gained more strength than the stronger leg [11].

Very little research looks at people over 40 specifically. One small, non-randomized study of 27 adults aged 40 to 56 found better dynamic balance after four weeks of single-leg Romanian deadlifts [12]. The sample was small and the study short. Most of the other studies above used young athletes, and the research cannot tell you how much each style helps a 45-year-old with a desk job.

## Unilateral vs. bilateral: side by side

| | Bilateral | Unilateral |
|---|---|---|
| Muscle growth | Similar | Similar |
| Strength in two-limb lifts | Higher | Lower |
| Strength in single-limb lifts | Lower | Higher |
| Balance demand | Lower | Higher |
| Side-to-side differences | Can persist or grow | Easier to see and correct |
| Time per session | Shorter | Longer, since you do each side |
| Best fit | Heavy strength work | Fixing imbalances, joint-friendly volume |

## How the Matrix uses both

The Matrix trains squat, hinge, push, and pull in every session, and each of those patterns can be trained with two limbs or one. The research above gives a simple rule for choosing. Use bilateral versions when you want maximum two-limb strength. Use unilateral versions when you want to find and fix side-to-side differences, or when a two-limb version bothers a joint.

## Common mistakes

**Choosing unilateral work because it feels harder.** A harder-feeling exercise is not always a better one. Balance can become the limit on a single-limb lift before the target muscle does. Pick exercises that let you train close to your limit safely.

**Skipping bilateral lifts completely.** The research shows two-limb training builds two-limb strength best [1][2]. If heavy squats, deadlifts, and presses are part of your goal, keep them in.

**Ignoring your weaker side.** Start sets on your weaker side and match reps on the stronger side. This keeps the stronger side from setting the pace.

**Adding single-limb work without adding recovery.** Doing each side doubles the sets and the time. Count both sides when you tally your weekly volume.

**Doing every set at the same style.** You can use both. Several of the studies above used a mix, and the evidence does not support an all-or-nothing rule.

## How to choose

Ask what you need from the next four weeks of training. If you need top-end strength in a barbell lift, keep that lift bilateral. If you notice one side lags, or a two-limb version bothers your back, hips, or knees, add a unilateral version. If you want a simple plan, use bilateral lifts for heavy work and unilateral lifts for higher-rep work.

## FAQ

### Is unilateral training better for muscle growth?

The research does not show that. Meta-analyses found no meaningful difference in muscle growth between unilateral and bilateral training [1][2].

### Does single-leg training make my squat stronger?

It can help, since strength carries over between related exercises [7]. Training the squat itself builds squat strength best [1][2].

### Will unilateral training fix my imbalance?

It can help. Trials in athletes found unilateral training reduced differences between legs [9][10]. A study of older women found a regular bilateral program also narrowed the gap [11]. Your starting point and your lifting history matter.

### Is single-leg work safer for my back?

Single-leg exercises usually use lighter total loads, which can reduce stress on your spine. This post found no trial that measured back pain or injury, so treat this as a practical point from coaching and not a research result.

### Do I need to do both?

Most people do well with a mix. Use bilateral lifts to build strength in the big patterns, and use unilateral lifts to cover weak spots and give joints a different kind of load.

## The bottom line

Unilateral and bilateral training build similar muscle. Each builds the most strength in the way you train it. A mix gives you the strength of two-limb lifts and the side-to-side control of single-limb work.
