You've probably noticed it. The program that used to work — the one from a magazine, a buddy, or an app — doesn't hit the same anymore. You do the work. You show up. And the results just aren't there like they used to be.

Most guys blame age. Or motivation. Or "just needing to try harder."

Usually it's neither. It's that you've been training against your own wiring the whole time — and at 25, your recovery capacity was big enough to cover for it. At 45, it isn't.

## What Neurotype Training Actually Is

Neurotype training comes from work by strength coach Christian Thibaudeau, built around a simple idea: not everyone's nervous system responds to training the same way.

Some guys thrive on high volume and predictable structure. Others burn out on that exact setup and only respond when there's variety, intensity, and something new to chase. Push the wrong style on the wrong nervous system, and you get two outcomes — either nothing happens, or you get results for a while and then flame out hard.

This isn't about personality tests or motivation. It's about how your nervous system actually responds to stress, novelty, and load — and it's testable, not guesswork.

## Why This Mattered Less At 25

At 25, most guys can out-recover a bad program match. Sleep is easier to come by, hormone production is higher, and the body just absorbs more punishment before it complains. You could run a program that fought your wiring for months and still see progress — slower than it should've been, but progress.

That buffer shrinks with age. Recovery capacity drops. Sleep gets more fragile. The margin for "training that fights your nervous system" gets thinner every year — and by 40, for a lot of guys, that margin's gone.

This is why the exact program that built you in your 20s can leave you flat, sore, and stuck in your 40s. It's not that the program got worse. It's that you no longer have the recovery buffer to survive fighting your own wiring.

## The Broad Strokes Of The System

Thibaudeau's framework breaks lifters into a handful of neurotypes, generally falling into two camps:

**Guys who need structure and volume.** They respond best to programs that build gradually, with predictable progression and enough total work to drive adaptation. Constant novelty actually works against them — it never lets any one stimulus build momentum.

**Guys who need intensity and variety.** They plateau fast on repetitive programs and respond much better to frequent changes in exercises, rep ranges, and intensity techniques. More volume isn't the answer for them — more stimulation is.

Most men fall somewhere between these poles, with a lean toward one side. The point isn't to memorize a label. It's to stop guessing and start training the nervous system you actually have.

## What This Looks Like In Practice

A guy who needs structure but keeps getting handed "muscle confusion" programs with a different workout every session never builds momentum — every stimulus gets abandoned before it does anything.

A guy who needs variety but is stuck running the same 5x5 for the sixth month straight isn't disciplined, he's stagnant, and his numbers show it.

Neither of these men is broken. They're both running someone else's program instead of their own blueprint.

## FAQs

**Can my neurotype change over time?**
The underlying wiring is fairly stable, but how it shows up can shift with stress, sleep, and recovery status. That's part of why programming should adjust over time, not stay static.

**Is this the same as a personality test?**
No. It's based on how your nervous system responds to training stimulus, not how you'd answer questions about your personality.

**Do I need testing to know my type?**
A proper assessment gives you a much clearer picture than guessing. Most guys can identify a rough lean on their own, but the details matter for actual programming.

**Does this replace good programming fundamentals?**
No — it sits on top of them. You still need progressive overload, adequate protein, and consistent training. Neurotype just tells you how to structure those fundamentals so they actually stick.

## The Bottom Line

Your training didn't stop working because you got older. It stopped working because the gap between "generic program" and "what your nervous system actually needs" finally got too big to out-recover.

Train the engine you actually have, not the one from a magazine cover. That's the difference between grinding through your 40s and actually building through them.
